Where your question goes

You type a sentence into Claude or ChatGPT and a paragraph types itself back. In between: a tokenizer, a few thousand kilometres of fibre, a scheduler, eight GPUs, a cache, and a loop that runs once per word. Step 1 of 8 · your device

Words become numbers before they leave your hand

Think of it as

Your sentence is cut into small pieces, like words on fridge magnets, and every piece has a number. The app puts your pieces in an envelope together with its own instructions and a note about you, the things you told it in settings, then seals it and sends it off.

You typed a sentence. The app does not send sentences. It chops your text into tokens: chunks of roughly three or four characters that the model has a numbered vocabulary for. Your question becomes about a dozen of them.

The app also bundles in things you never see. A system prompt describing how the assistant should behave, and every earlier message in this chat. All of it is packed into one request, encrypted, and sent.

This is where you enter the picture. Anything you told the app about yourself in settings, that you are a software developer or a teacher, where you live, how you like answers phrased, plus any facts it saved from earlier chats, is written into the system prompt right here, as ordinary tokens at the front of the request. The model never looks you up. It only ever sees what the app pasted in.

Internals
Your profile
Custom instructions, preferences and "memory" facts are assembled by the app into the system prompt, often as a labelled block after the provider's own instructions, together with today's date and your locale. Some apps first run a retrieval step to pick which saved facts are relevant to this question. Either way the result is text, sent every turn, sitting in the cached prefix (step 8) until you edit your settings.
Tokenizer
Byte-pair encoding over a vocabulary of roughly 100k to 200k entries. A leading space is part of the token, so " is" and "is" are different ids. Rare words split into several tokens; common ones are one. The ids shown on the stage are illustrative, the split is approximate.
Request body
One JSON document: model, system, a messages[] array of alternating user and assistant turns, max_tokens, and stream: true so the answer can arrive piecewise.
Size
A few kilobytes for a fresh chat. Hundreds of kilobytes for a long one, because the whole history travels again every turn. Step 8 is about why.
Transport
HTTPS over TLS 1.3. One handshake, then the request is a single POST whose response stays open while the answer streams.
Step 2 of 8 · the wire

Your question crosses the country faster than you blink

Think of it as

The envelope goes through your Wi-Fi and along glass cables to a building far away, in less time than a blink. At the door, a guard checks that it is really you and points you to the right room.

The encrypted request hops from your Wi-Fi to your internet provider, then onto long-haul fibre where light carries it at about two thirds of its speed in a vacuum.

It lands at an edge point of presence near a data center. There the provider's API gateway opens the encryption, checks who you are and how much you are allowed to send, and routes you to a region that is running the model you asked for. The whole trip takes tens of milliseconds, a fraction of the time you will wait for the first word.

Internals
Anycast
The API's IP address is announced from many locations at once. Internet routing delivers you to the closest edge, not a single origin.
TLS termination
The edge decrypts, so everything inside the provider's network is plain HTTP over private links.
Auth and limits
API key or session cookie resolves to an organization and a tier. Rate limits are enforced per organization on requests per minute and tokens per minute. Over the limit means a 429 before any GPU is touched.
Routing
Pick a region and cluster that has the requested model loaded and has capacity. If none does, the request waits in a queue here rather than at the GPU.
Latency budget
Typically 20 to 80 ms one way. This is the smallest slice of what you wait for; the model itself is the rest.
Step 3 of 8 · the data center

You share the machine with strangers

Think of it as

Inside is a huge kitchen with many identical stoves. A host seats you at one that has space. Your order is cooked together with dozens of other people's orders on the same stove. That is why it is cheap. The pans never touch: nobody else's order ends up on your plate, and yours never lands on theirs.

The building holds rows of racks, each full of GPUs, with copies of the model, called replicas, loaded on many of them. A scheduler picks a replica with room for you.

Your request does not run alone. It is merged into a batch with dozens of other people's requests that arrive around the same moment, because running a batch costs almost the same as running one request. This sharing is why a query costs cents rather than dollars.

Internals
Replica
One loaded copy of the model on one node, or a few nodes for the largest models. A cluster serves a model with tens to thousands of replicas.
Continuous batching
New requests join the running batch between decode steps and finished ones leave. Nobody waits for the slowest sequence to finish before the next batch starts.
Scheduler goals
Keep the GPUs busy, keep queue time low, and never let the batch's KV cache (step 5) overflow GPU memory. Those three fight each other.
Physical scale
A rack of eight-GPU nodes draws about 120 kW and is liquid cooled. A site is 100 MW to over 1 GW. Your request touches one rack for about a second.
Queue time
Single-digit milliseconds when the region is quiet. It is the first thing to grow when a region is hot.
Does sharing the batch expose my question?
  1. No, by designRequests in a batch share the GPU's arithmetic, not each other's data. Each request has its own KV cache blocks, and attention is masked so a token can only see tokens from its own request. Your answer depends on your tokens and the weights, nothing else.
  2. Nothing is learned nowWeights are frozen while your request runs. Nothing you type changes the model that is answering, and nothing can cross into another request in the same batch.
  3. But later, maybeOn free and consumer tiers, unless you opt out, the logged conversation and your reactions to it can be collected and used months later to train a future model version. That is a separate, offline pipeline. See where your data actually goes.
Step 4 of 8 · the GPU node

The model is too big for one chip

Think of it as

The model is a book too heavy for one person, so eight helpers each hold one part and pass notes to each other very quickly. The slow part is not the thinking. It is turning the pages.

A frontier model's weights, the numbers learned in training, run to hundreds of gigabytes. No single chip holds that, so the model is sliced across the eight GPUs in one server, each keeping one eighth in its high-bandwidth memory (HBM). Links between the GPUs are fast enough that they behave like one large chip.

The surprising fact about generating text: the limit is how fast weights can be read out of memory, not how much arithmetic the chips can do. Every token that comes out requires reading the whole model once.

Internals
Tensor parallelism
Each layer's weight matrices are split column- or row-wise across the 8 GPUs. After each layer an all-reduce over NVLink combines the partial results.
HBM
144 to 288 GB per GPU in 2026 parts, at 5 to 8 TB/s. This bandwidth, divided by the bytes of weights, sets the ceiling on tokens per second at small batch sizes.
NVLink
About 1.8 TB/s per GPU between the eight. Node-to-node traffic goes over InfiniBand or Ethernet at a tenth of that, which is why a model is kept inside one node when it fits.
Precision
Weights stored as FP8 or bf16, one or two bytes per parameter. A 400B-parameter model at FP8 is about 400 GB, or 50 GB per GPU.
Why batching
At batch size 1, each decode step reads every weight to produce one token. At batch size 64 it reads them once for 64 tokens. Compute is nearly free until the batch is large. That is the economics of step 3.
Step 5 of 8 · prefill

The model reads your whole question at once

Think of it as

The helpers read your whole question in one go and write a sticky note about every word, so they never have to read it again. That is the pause before the answer starts.

Now the model runs. In its first pass, called prefill, it processes all your tokens simultaneously, layer by layer. Within each layer every token looks back at every earlier token to decide what matters. That mechanism is attention.

As it goes it writes down a compact set of notes about each token, called the KV cache, so it never has to re-read the question. Prefill ends when the first token of the answer is ready. The pause before a reply starts to appear is this pass.

The profile tokens from step 1 are read here too, by the same attention. "Software developer" and "prefers concise answers" are simply earlier tokens that every later token can look back at. That is the whole mechanism by which your settings shape the reply.

Internals
Q, K, V
In each layer, each token's vector is projected into a Query, a Key and a Value. The attention weight from token i to token j is softmax(Q_i · K_j / √d), computed only for j ≤ i (causal). The output for i is the weighted sum of the V_j.
KV cache
Every K and V, for every token, at every layer, is stored so decode steps can attend to the prompt without recomputing it.
bytes per token ≈ 2 (K and V) × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × bytes_per_value
Worked size
80 layers, 8 KV heads of 128 (grouped-query attention), bf16: 2 × 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 320 KB per token. Your prompt is a few megabytes. A 100k-token context is about 32 GB, on top of the weights. This is what limits how many requests fit in one batch.
Cost profile
Prefill is compute-bound and parallel, so it is fast per token but grows with prompt length. Time to first token is mostly prefill plus queue time: 200 ms to a couple of seconds.
Step 6 of 8 · decode

One token at a time, sent the instant it exists

Think of it as

Then they write the answer one word at a time, checking their sticky notes for each one, and hand every word to the messenger the moment it is written. That is why the answer appears word by word.

The model produces the answer one token per loop. For each: read the KV cache, score every entry in the vocabulary, pick one, append it to the cache, repeat. A sixty-word answer is about eighty loops of a few milliseconds each.

Each new token is pushed back down the wire immediately. That is why the answer types out in front of you rather than appearing whole. The model is still writing while you are already reading.

Internals
One step
A forward pass over a single new token per sequence in the batch. Attention reads the whole KV cache for that sequence. Output is a vector of logits, one per vocabulary entry.
Sampling
Logits divided by temperature, softmaxed, then truncated by top-p or top-k, then one id drawn. Temperature 0 means take the arg max every time.
Speed
50 to 150 tokens per second per sequence. Each step re-reads all the weights, so this is bounded by HBM bandwidth and shared across the batch.
Streaming
Each token, or small group, is sent as a server-sent event on the still-open HTTP response.
Reasoning models
May decode thousands of hidden tokens before the first visible one. Same loop, longer wait.
Speculative decoding
A small draft model guesses several tokens ahead; the large model verifies them all in one pass. Accepted guesses come out for the price of one step.
Step 7 of 8 · back on your screen

The stream is stitched back into text

Think of it as

Your app catches the words as they arrive and lays them out into a tidy message. The last note says "done" and how many words were used.

Your app receives a trickle of small events, each carrying a fragment of text. It appends them to the message bubble as they arrive and renders formatting, headings and lists, as soon as it can tell what they are.

The final event says why the model stopped, because it finished or hit the length limit, and how many tokens went in and came out. That count is what the bill is based on.

Internals
Server-sent events
Content type text/event-stream. Each event is an event: line and a data: line of JSON. Deltas are text fragments; they need not align with tokens or words.
Rendering
Markdown is re-parsed on every delta, with care for an unclosed code fence or list mid-stream. Good clients render optimistically and fix up when the closing marker arrives.
Terminal events
stop_reason is one of end_turn, max_tokens, stop_sequence, or tool_use. usage reports input tokens, output tokens, and cache reads and writes.
Tool use
If the model decided to call a tool, the stream ends with a structured request instead of prose. The app runs the tool and sends a new request with the result appended. Same round trip, again.
Step 8 of 8 · your next question

The model remembers nothing. The app does.

5 tokens
Think of it as

The helpers forget everything the moment they finish. When you ask another question, your app hands over the whole conversation again. Because the beginning is the same as last time, the kitchen reuses its old sticky notes and only reads the new part.

You ask a follow-up. The model has no memory of the exchange it just finished. The moment the last token left, it forgot you.

What makes the conversation continue is your app. It re-sends the entire transcript, system prompt, first question, the answer and the new question, on every turn. That is why long chats slow down and cost more.

The provider softens this with a prompt cache. The start of the transcript is identical to last turn, so the server reuses the KV cache it built then and only processes the new part. Eventually the transcript hits the context window limit, and the app has to summarize or drop old turns.

Internals
Stateless inference
Every API call is independent. Server-side state after a call is nothing but the cache below and a usage log.
Prompt caching
Exact prefix match on the token sequence. Cached KV blocks live in HBM or host memory for a time-to-live of about five minutes by default, longer on some tiers. Cache reads are billed at roughly a tenth of the normal input price, cache writes a little above it.
Turn two
Prefill covers only the uncached suffix, so time to first token drops from hundreds of milliseconds to tens. Anything that changes the prefix, a timestamp in the system prompt say, defeats the cache.
Context window
200k to 1M tokens. Usage grows by every turn's input plus output. When full, apps truncate the oldest turns, compact them into a summary, or retrieve relevant past turns instead of sending all of them.
"Memory" features
A store of facts about you, injected into the system prompt. Useful, but from the model's side it is just more tokens in the request. Because the profile block sits at the very front and rarely changes, it is the most reliably cached part of every request; editing your settings invalidates the cache once, then it is warm again.
Three caches, one process
  1. KV cacheInside one request (steps 5 and 6). Every engine has it, open or closed. Not optional, never billed on its own.
  2. Prefix cacheAcross requests (step 8). Reuses the KV of an identical token prefix. Anthropic has you mark cache breakpoints and bills reads at a discount; OpenAI applies it automatically above about a thousand tokens; vLLM and SGLang do it automatically and for free on hardware you own. A hit changes only step 5.
  3. Answer cacheBefore the model (skips steps 2 to 6). An app-level store of whole answers keyed on similar questions. Chat products do not do this, since the same question in a different conversation deserves a different answer. FAQ bots and internal tools do.

Other outputs · text, code and images

The language of the machine is numbers.

Whatever you ask for, the model in the middle does one thing: multiply and add very large tables of numbers. Everything you give it is turned into numbers first, and everything it returns is numbers turned back into something you can read or see. What changes by medium is the translator at each end, and how the output loop runs.

Sound, video and spreadsheets go the same way: samples, frames and cells become numbers, the model runs, and a decoder turns numbers back into the medium. The model never sees a word, a bracket or a colour. It sees positions in very long lists.

What differs by medium

Code generation

Identical to what you just scrolled. Code is text; the tokenizer has entries for def, { and four spaces of indentation like any other fragment. The model decodes one token at a time and streams them. The only differences are in the app: it renders into a code block, may run a linter, and in agent tools it feeds compiler or test output back as the next user turn. Same round trip, run in a loop.

Image generation, diffusion

Steps 1 to 4 are the same: your prompt is tokenized, travels the wire, lands in a batch on a GPU node. Then a text encoder turns the prompt into a conditioning vector once, and the model runs 20 to 50 denoising steps over a grid of noise, each step a full pass that nudges every pixel at once. There is no word-by-word loop and no KV cache. A decoder turns the final grid into pixels and one file comes back, not a stream. Some apps show a blurry preview refining, which is those steps.

Image generation, image tokens

Newer multimodal models treat an image as a long sequence of visual tokens and generate it with the same autoregressive decode loop as text, patch by patch, then a decoder renders the patches. This is closer to the page you scrolled, only with thousands of tokens per image and a different vocabulary. Either way, the image itself is not kept in the text KV cache across turns; the app re-attaches it, or its token form, as part of the transcript.

Privacy · where your data actually is

The stranger in the batch is not the risk. Storage is.

Step 3 puts your request on a GPU with dozens of other people's. That part is isolated by design. The places where a personal question or a tax return can actually be seen are before and after the GPU, and they depend on the product tier and settings you use.

Along the journey

  1. lowStep 2 · the wireEncrypted in transit, decrypted at the provider's edge. Inside their network it is plaintext. Your trust in the provider begins here.
  2. lowSteps 3 to 6 · the GPUsYour tokens live in GPU memory for the length of the request, in cache blocks only your request can address, then the blocks are freed. Weights do not change during inference, so nothing you send can appear in anyone else's answer from this model. Training, where it happens, is the offline path below.
  3. lowStep 8 · the prefix cacheScoped to your account or organization. Another customer's identical prompt does not hit your entries. Researchers have shown timing side channels on shared caches; scoping is the defence.
  4. realLogs and retentionRequests and responses are usually stored for a period for abuse monitoring and debugging. Consumer chat history stays until you delete it. API, business and enterprise tiers typically offer short or zero retention. A court order can override deletion, as one did in 2025.
  5. realTrainingFree and consumer tiers may use conversations, and your reactions to answers, to improve future models unless you opt out. API, business and enterprise tiers generally exclude training by contract. Check the setting; do not assume. The path that data takes is drawn below.
  6. realHuman reviewUnder abuse and safety policies, staff can read conversations that are flagged.
  7. realMemory featuresIf the app saves "files jointly, two dependents" as a memory, that fact rides in step 1 of every future request until you delete it.
  8. realApp bugsThe one well-known cross-user leak, other people's chat titles appearing in a sidebar in 2023, was a web-app caching bug, not the GPU pipeline.

If you are sharing a tax return

  1. Check data controlsTurn off "improve the model" or use a plan that excludes training by default.
  2. Prefer short retentionBusiness, enterprise or API tiers with zero-retention options. Temporary or incognito chat modes skip history and memory.
  3. Redact what adds nothingSocial security numbers, account numbers, dependents' full names. The analysis does not need them.
  4. Manage memoryTurn it off for that conversation, or delete the memories it creates afterwards.
  5. Or keep it on your machineRun an open-weight model locally. Nothing leaves the device. The trade is a smaller model.

Policies and retention windows differ by product and change over time. Verify the current terms of the product you use rather than relying on this page.

If training is on: the offline path your conversation takes

This is the part the inference journey never shows, because it happens on different machines, weeks or months later. The shape below is the typical pipeline described in provider policies and research papers. Exact stores, windows and filters differ by lab.

1

Logged at the gateway

The request and the streamed response are written to a logging store, keyed by conversation and account id, with model, timestamps and token counts. This is the same log used for abuse monitoring and debugging. Deleting a chat removes it here, after a delay.

2

Your reactions join it

Thumbs up or down, "regenerate", copying an answer, editing your question and asking again, choosing between two answers. In coding assistants: whether you accepted, edited or rejected a suggestion. These events are stored as a feedback log tied to the same conversation id.

3

Eligibility filter

Only conversations whose account and tier permit training pass. Opt-outs, temporary chats, API, business and enterprise traffic are dropped here. This is the gate your settings control.

4

Scrubbing and sampling

Automated removal of names, emails, numbers that look like identifiers, then deduplication and quality and safety filters. A sample may be read and labelled by reviewers. Your tax return is meant to lose its identifiers here; the figures and the question survive.

5

Becomes training data

Answers you liked become examples to imitate. Pairs where you preferred one answer over another become preference data for reinforcement learning. Hard cases become evaluation sets. Datasets are versioned and kept under their own retention rules, separate from your chat history.

6

Baked into a future model

A training run on a separate cluster produces the next model version, released weeks or months later. From then on the influence is diffuse and cannot be removed by deleting the chat. Models rarely reproduce a single conversation, but this is why the gate at step 3 is the one that matters.

Coding assistants. Tools such as Cursor and GitHub Copilot have shipped with telemetry that collects prompts, code snippets and accept-or-reject signals unless a privacy mode or opt-out is set, with business tiers excluded by default. Whether that is on for you today is a setting to check, not a fact to assume.

What "delete" reaches. Steps 1 and 2 within the retention window. Not step 5's dataset snapshots once taken, and never step 6.

Caveats

What is simplified here

  • Numbers are typical of frontier-model serving in 2026: GPUs with 144 to 288 GB of HBM, models of a few hundred billion parameters, 80-ish layers with grouped-query attention. Real deployments differ by vendor, model and day.
  • The tokenizer on the stage splits on words and punctuation and breaks long words in two. Real byte-pair tokenizers split differently and the ids shown are illustrative.
  • Some providers shard a model across more than one node, use mixture-of-experts layers, or run prefill and decode on separate machines. The shape of the journey is the same.
  • Nothing here calls any API. The answer text is a fixed example.
