When an assistant acts for you

You text an assistant from your messaging app and, a minute later, your calendar has changed and an email has gone out. Between those two moments: a relay, a webhook, a token vault, copies of your calendar and inbox inside a request, four model calls, a question back to you, and two API calls made in your name. This page follows one message the whole way, and answers the five things people are usually unsure about.

1 / 8 · Message
Step 1 of 8 · your messaging app

"End-to-end" ends at the agent

Think of it as

You hand a sealed letter to a courier. The courier cannot read it. But the person it is addressed to can, and here that person is the assistant's computer, not a friend. The letter is safe on the way and open on arrival.

You type into the messaging app you already use. The message is encrypted on your phone and carried by the platform, Apple or Meta or Slack, to the number or bot you are texting.

The number belongs to the agent. End-to-end encryption means only the two ends can read the message, and the agent's server is the other end. So the provider's computer reads your words in plaintext. It has to, or it could not act on them. The platform in the middle sees who messaged whom and when, and for many bot channels the content too. Encryption protects the trip, not the destination.

Internals
iMessage
Encrypted device-to-device between Apple users. A business or bot is reached through Messages for Business: encrypted from your device to Apple, then delivered to the business's endpoint over TLS. Apple is a relay, the business is the end.
WhatsApp
Personal chats are end-to-end. Chats with a business on the Cloud API terminate at Meta-hosted infrastructure, or at the business's own server on the on-premises API. Meta's documentation says as much; the business, or the vendor running its integration, reads plaintext.
Slack, Telegram bots, SMS
Not end-to-end at all. The platform reads content, stores it, and hands it to the bot over HTTPS.
Metadata
Sender, recipient, timestamps, message sizes and delivery receipts are visible to the platform in every case, encrypted content or not.
What this means
Your threat model for an agent is the provider's security and policy, not the transport. The transport is fine.
Step 2 of 8 · the provider's inbox

Your message lands in a database before it reaches a model

Think of it as

The letter arrives at an office. A clerk checks who it is from, pulls your folder from the filing cabinet, which holds every past letter and the notes they kept about you, and clips the new letter to the front.

The platform delivers your message to the provider as a webhook, a web request to a URL they registered. An orchestrator, the program that runs the agent, looks up which account the sender belongs to and loads your conversation.

This is the first place your data is stored. The message, the earlier turns, the agent's notes about you, and the keys to your connected accounts all live in the provider's database. It is encrypted on disk with the provider's own keys, which means the provider, and anyone with the right access inside it, can read it. That is normal and unavoidable for a service that acts on your behalf. It is also the thing to ask about before connecting your email.

Internals
Webhook
HTTPS POST from the platform with sender id, message body, attachments and a signature the provider verifies. Retried on failure, so the inbox must be idempotent.
Account resolution
Phone number or platform user id → your account, plan and entitlements. This is also where rate limits and abuse checks run.
Conversation store
Typically a Postgres row per message plus an object store for attachments, encrypted at rest with cloud KMS keys the provider controls. Retention is a policy choice: often the life of the account, sometimes shorter for content.
Token vault
OAuth refresh tokens for your calendar and email, stored separately, encrypted with a key that only the orchestrator's service role can use. A leak here is the worst case, because tokens act as you.
Staff access
Governed by policy and audit logs, not by cryptography. Look for statements about access controls, SOC 2 reports and whether support staff can read conversation content.
Step 3 of 8 · context assembly

Copies of your calendar and inbox go into the request

Think of it as

Before answering, the clerk opens your diary, skims your recent letters with Priya, and glances at the alarm board. They copy the relevant bits onto one sheet, together with your letter and the office rulebook, and hand that sheet to the expert in the back room.

The model cannot browse your accounts. The orchestrator does it for the model. Using the keys you granted when you connected each account, it fetches tomorrow's calendar, recent email threads that mention Priya, and any open Sentry alert, and pastes the results into one request alongside the agent's instructions, the list of tools it may use, its memory of you, and your message.

This is the step people don't picture. Your data has now been copied out of Google's or Microsoft's systems into a text document that will travel to a model. The request is a few thousand tokens before the model has done anything. Every extra connected account makes it larger and slower, and adds to what the next two companies see.

Internals
OAuth
When you connected Calendar, you approved scopes such as calendar.events and gmail.send. The provider holds a refresh token and mints short-lived access tokens per call. Revoking access at Google or Microsoft kills the token everywhere at once.
Retrieval
Cheap heuristics first: events in a time window, threads matching a name, open issues. Heavier setups embed your mail and do vector search. Either way, only what is retrieved is sent; the rest stays where it was.
Tool definitions
JSON schemas describing each action the model may request, sent with every call. Twenty tools cost one to two thousand tokens before any content. The Model Context Protocol standardises this shape.
Request size
Instructions and tools around 2,400 tokens, memories about 300, retrieved context about 1,500, your message. Roughly 4,500 tokens for the first call in this example.
Step 4 of 8 · the model call

A second company sees the request

Think of it as

The office does not employ the expert. It sends the sheet to an expert's office across town, waits for the reply, and files a copy of both. Two offices now have your letter.

Most agent providers do not train or host their own model. The orchestrator sends the assembled request to a model API run by another company, and the whole Prompt journey happens there: wire, batching, prefill, decode, streaming back. Compressed on the stage to one row.

The consequence is that two companies now hold your data under two policies. The provider keeps the transcript for as long as its terms say. The model API keeps request logs for its own window, often zero to thirty days for business customers, and under API terms does not train on them. Both are worth reading; the second one is easy to forget because you never chose it.

Internals
The call
One HTTPS POST to the model API from the provider's servers with the full request, tools array included. The response is either text or a structured tool call. Streaming is often disabled inside agent loops because the orchestrator needs the complete tool call before acting.
Retention at the model API
API traffic typically defaults to a short abuse-monitoring window and no training; zero-data-retention agreements exist for larger customers. Ask the provider which tier they are on.
Prompt caching
The instructions and tool schemas are identical on every call, so a prefix cache at the model API makes loops 2 to 4 cheaper and faster. The Prompt page's step 8 is doing real work here.
Region
The provider's servers and the model API may be in different countries. Data residency claims need to cover both.
Step 5 of 8 · the tool loop

One text becomes four model calls

Think of it as

The expert does not do things. They write instructions: "look up Friday", "find a free slot", "draft the email". The clerk runs each errand, comes back with the result, and the expert reads everything again before writing the next instruction. Four trips for one request.

The model's first reply is not an answer. It is a tool call: read the calendar for tomorrow. The orchestrator runs it with your token, appends the result to the request, and calls the model again. Now it asks to move the event. Again. Now it drafts the email. Again. Finally it writes the reply to you.

Each loop is a full model call on a request that has grown by the last result. That is where the time and the compute go: four calls of three to five seconds each, on 5,000 then 7,000 then 9,000 then 11,000 tokens, instead of the one call a chat answer takes. A request that "just moves a meeting" is a small program executed one step at a time.

Internals
Loop shape
Call model → if response contains tool calls, execute them (often in parallel), append tool_result blocks, call again → until the model returns plain text or a step limit is hit. Providers cap loops at 10 to 30 to bound cost.
Context growth
Every tool result stays in the transcript for subsequent calls. Verbose results (a full inbox page) balloon the context; good orchestrators truncate and summarise tool output before appending.
Time per loop
Prefill on the growing context plus decode of a few hundred tokens of tool call, plus the tool's own latency: a calendar read is fast, an email search can take seconds.
Failure handling
A tool error is appended as a result and the model is asked to recover. Retries multiply cost; runaway loops are the classic agent bill shock.
Step 6 of 8 · the human in the loop

Before it sends, it asks. The human is you.

Think of it as

Before posting the letter to Priya, the clerk knocks on your door: "Friday at ten, and I'll tell her. Yes?" Nothing moves until you nod. Managers in the office can read the file afterwards, but they do not get to nod for you.

Moving your own event is reversible. Emailing Priya is not. Well-built agents draw that line: before an irreversible or outward-facing action they stop and send you a confirmation in the chat. The loop pauses, the tokens sit idle, and nothing runs until you tap.

Two things are often confused here. The human in the loop is you, not the provider's staff. Staff can read conversation logs under the provider's access policy, but they are not reviewing or approving actions in real time. And the gate is a product decision, not a property of the model. Some products skip it for speed. Whether yours asks before sending, paying or deleting is something to test on day one.

Internals
Gated actions
Anything that leaves your account or cannot be undone: send email or message, make a payment, delete, share externally, post publicly. Reads and internal edits usually run without asking.
Mechanics
The tool call is parked in the database with a pending state; a message with the proposed action goes to you; your reply resolves it; the loop resumes with the tool result. Timeouts expire pending actions after hours or days.
Standing approvals
"Always allow emails to Priya" turns a gate into a rule. Convenient, and the point at which a misread instruction can act without you.
Staff access
Read access to logs for support and safety, governed by policy and audit trails. Distinct from any ability to approve or trigger actions on your accounts, which they should not have.
Step 7 of 8 · action and reply

Two API calls in your name, and a longer paper trail

Think of it as

The clerk moves the appointment in your diary, posts the letter to Priya, and sends you a note: "Done." Then they file everything: your letter, their errands, the reply, and a new note in your folder that says Priya prefers mornings.

With your approval, the orchestrator calls the Calendar API to move the event and the Email API to send the note, both authenticated as you. Your accounts show the change as if you had made it yourself. The confirmation travels back down the same messaging path to your phone.

Count what now exists. The transcript, the tool results (your calendar rows, your email threads), a new memory about Priya, and an audit entry, all at the provider. Request logs at the model API for its window. The moved event and the sent email in your own accounts, where they were always going to be. None of this is sinister. It is simply more copies in more places than a chat answer leaves behind, and worth knowing before you connect something sensitive.

Internals
Acting as you
The calls carry your OAuth access token, so the receiving service records you as the actor. Some providers add a footer or header identifying the agent; many do not.
Memory writes
The model or a side process extracts durable facts ("Priya prefers mornings", "user is in Boston") into a memory store injected into future requests. Review and delete these; they persist independently of the conversation.
Audit log
Which tool ran with which arguments, when, and on whose approval. Ask whether you can see yours. It is the record that matters if something went wrong.
Reply path
Provider → platform API → your device, encrypted per platform as in step 1, in reverse.
Step 8 of 8 · while you sleep

The agent runs without you, on permissions you granted once

Think of it as

At three in the morning the alarm board lights up. The clerk, who never sleeps, reads it, opens your folder, works out what to do, and leaves a note on your door. You gave them the office keys once; they use them whenever the alarm goes.

An agent connected to Sentry does not wait for you to text. An alert fires a webhook into the same inbox, the same orchestrator assembles context, the same loop runs, and a message appears on your phone at 3:12 am with a summary and a suggested fix. Scheduled runs work the same way: a morning briefing is a loop that starts on a timer.

This is compute spent, and data read, while nobody is watching. It is also the point of an agent. The trade is standing permissions: tokens that keep working, memories that keep accumulating, gates that you may have loosened. Know where the list of connected accounts is, in the provider's settings and in your Google or Microsoft account's third-party access page, because revoking there is the one switch that stops everything at once.

Internals
Triggers
Inbound webhooks (Sentry, GitHub, Stripe), cron schedules, and polling of accounts without webhooks. Each trigger is a job in the provider's queue that starts a loop with no user message.
Compute at rest
A daily briefing plus a handful of alerts is tens of model calls per day per user, on contexts as large as the interactive ones. This is the cost line that scales with users even when they are idle.
Refresh tokens
Long-lived by design; Google's expire after six months of inactivity, Microsoft's after ninety days, otherwise indefinitely until revoked. Rotating them is the provider's job; revoking them is yours.
Revocation
Google: myaccount.google.com → Security → Third-party apps. Microsoft: account.microsoft.com → Privacy → App access. Slack: workspace apps. These kill the token even if the provider's own settings page does not.

The five questions · short answers

If you only read one section

stored where

Transcript, tool results, memories, tokens and audit log at the provider. Request logs at the model API for its window. Metadata at the messaging platform. The changed event and sent email in your accounts.

encrypted

In transit everywhere. End-to-end only as far as the agent's server, which reads plaintext by design. At rest with the provider's keys, which means the provider can read it under its own policy.

human in the loop

You, at approval gates before irreversible actions, if the product has them. Provider staff can read logs under policy; they do not approve actions. Test the gate on day one.

how long

Tens of seconds for a small task: delivery, context fetch, four model calls on a growing context, tool latency, and however long you take to approve.

compute

Several model calls per message, each larger than the last, plus runs you never see: alerts and schedules spend compute while you sleep. Tens of cents per task, not a fraction of one.

The bill

What this one task cost to run

Model list prices as on the Prompt page, $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output. The provider pays this and charges you a subscription that has to cover it, including the runs you never see.

Caveats

What is simplified here

  • "The provider" is a composite of agents you message that act on your accounts. Real products differ in whether they host their own model, where the gate sits, and how long they keep data. The shape of the journey is the same.
  • Encryption claims per platform are drawn from the platforms' own documentation as of 2026 and can change. Verify for the app you use.
  • Token counts, times and prices are illustrative and rounded. Nothing here calls any API.
